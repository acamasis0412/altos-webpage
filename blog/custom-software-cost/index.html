What Does Custom Software Actually Cost? (And What Drives the Price)

AC
Al Camasis
Founder, Altos · October 5, 2026 · 4 min read

"How much does custom software cost?" is the first question almost everyone asks us. The honest answer, "it depends," is also the least satisfying one. So instead of dodging it, here is exactly what it depends on, where the money usually goes, and how to get a real number for your project.

Why there is no sticker price

Custom software is built around your business, so asking what it costs is a bit like asking what a house costs. Two projects that are both called "an inventory system" can differ in cost by ten times, depending on what they actually need to do. Anyone who quotes you a firm number before understanding your business is guessing, or planning to make it up later in change requests.

The five things that actually drive the price

  • Scope. How many screens, workflows, and types of user the system needs. This is the biggest driver by far.
  • Integrations. Every system it has to talk to, such as your webstore, payments, or accounting, adds work. How messy those systems are to connect to matters more than how many there are.
  • Your existing data. Moving years of records out of spreadsheets or an old system cleanly is real work, and it is easy to underestimate.
  • How reliable it has to be. A tool your whole business runs on needs more testing and more edge cases handled than an internal report nobody depends on.
  • Who builds it. The size and seniority of the team, and how much overhead comes along with them.

Where the money usually leaks

In our experience, most of the cost in a typical project is not engineering. It is overhead: large teams that spend their time coordinating, junior developers learning on your budget, rework caused by decisions nobody made clearly, and cheap builds that have to be rebuilt a year later. The cheapest quote is often the most expensive project, because you pay for it twice.

How to keep the cost down without cutting corners

  • Start small. Build the smallest version that solves the real problem, then grow it once it is earning its keep.
  • Describe the outcome, not the features. "We keep overselling" gives a good team room to find the simplest fix. A long feature wishlist does not.
  • Keep what already works. If your webstore works, connect to it rather than rebuilding it.
  • Choose a lean, senior team. Experienced engineers make the right calls the first time, and AI now handles much of the repetitive work that used to drive up the bill. We wrote about why that combination costs less in How AI Lets a Lean Senior Team Ship Big Products Faster and Cheaper.

How to get a real number

The fastest way to an honest estimate is a short conversation. What are you trying to fix? What systems does it need to touch? What would success look like six months from now? From there, we come back with a scoped estimate and a few options at different sizes, so you can choose what fits your budget rather than being handed one take-it-or-leave-it figure.

If you are weighing up a project and want a real number instead of "it depends," tell us what you are trying to build.
